Take one

Shots save like any download, to wherever your browser keeps them. They're the visuals only, at the size they're drawn; the panel, buttons and labels are left out. A quick flash and a note show each one saving, except in full screen, so nothing appears in a recording.

How it listens

While a track plays, the page measures the sound many times a second and turns it into three levels, shown live on the What it hears meters:

Bass
The kick drum and bassline. Most backgrounds punch, swell, glow or speed up on it.
Mid
Vocals, synths and guitars. Usually shifts colours or adds a little extra movement.
High
Hi-hats, cymbals and sparkle. Brightens highlights, sparks and glitter.

Levels rise quickly and fall gently, so the picture pulses with the beat rather than flickering.

The full spectrum

A few backgrounds read the whole sound, not just three levels, so different parts of the picture follow different instruments:

  • Spectrum Mandala: each ring is a slice of the spectrum.
  • Fractal Animations: each colour is a slice of the spectrum.
  • Tentacle Fractal Tree: each tentacle follows its own frequency.
  • Milkdrop Feedback: draws the actual waveform as its ring.

Live input

Live input, at the top of the panel, makes the visuals react to a microphone or line-in instead of a file: a DJ set, a jam, or whatever's playing in the room. The browser asks once for permission. If you have more than one input, a menu lets you pick, and the Level slider boosts a quiet input or calms a loud one until the meters dance. You won't hear the input played back through the Lab. Browsers only allow this on a secure page, so if the Lab is opened from another computer over plain http, open the Lab file straight from your own computer instead.

Tip: play the music from a separate app (a media player such as VLC, DJ software, or a mixer’s line-out), or from a different browser, rather than from another tab in the same browser as the Lab. When a browser records its own playback, it can briefly lose the sound whenever that tab’s playback changes, and the visuals freeze. If the input ever goes completely silent while music should be playing, the Lab reconnects it by itself within a few seconds.

Everything happens in your browser. The music is never uploaded.

The effects

  1. 3D tiltThe picture leans like a screen seen at an angle and slowly rocks in two directions, adding depth. Music: none - it's a steady, slow drift that sits well under the other effects.
  2. Acid contour bandsBright stripes flow across the surfaces like oil on water. Each background lays them out in its own way. Music: they roll steadily, and some backgrounds push them along with the mids.
  3. Blacklight posterThe psychedelic posters of the 60s and 70s, printed in fluorescent inks on black flock and lit by ultraviolet. The dark parts of the picture become black velvet, every colour snaps to one of six day-glo inks (hot pink, orange, yellow, green, cyan and violet), anything white glows the violet of a UV tube, and everything bright gets a soft fluorescent glow. Music: the inks slowly drift round, faster with the mids, the kick pulses the glow, and a drop strobes the UV light for a moment.
  4. Blue TintSilent films were shot in black and white and coloured afterwards, scene by scene, to set the mood: blue for night scenes lit only by the moon, amber for scenes lit by lamps and candles. Labs did this two ways, and both are here. Tinting dyed the whole film, so the highlights take the colour and the blacks stay black: Azure is a pale moonlit blue and Nocturne a deep, darker night blue. Toning chemically turned the silver image itself blue in ferric ferrocyanide (Prussian blue), so the shadows and mid-tones go blue while the highlights stay clear: that is Prussian. Each holds for about 40 seconds and then dissolves into the next, with the gentle flicker and grain of an old print. Music: the bass deepens the flicker, and a drop glows the warm amber lamplight tint in for a few seconds, as if someone lit a lamp, before the night blue returns.
  5. Breathing zoomThe whole picture swells towards you and eases back, like it's breathing. Music: the deeper the bass, the bigger the breath.
  6. Cel shadingThe cartoon and anime look: every colour is snapped to a few flat brightness levels, so smooth shading turns into hard-edged bands, colours get punchier and bold ink outlines go round the shapes. Best on smooth backgrounds like Ocean, Fire Quartz, The Thing and Deep Sea Fractal. Music: the picture drops to fewer, bolder bands on each kick and the outlines thicken; a drop gives a two-tone "comic panel" moment before easing back.
  7. Changing symmetryFolds the picture into a kaleidoscope of 5, 6, 8 or 12 slices. Music: switches to a new number of slices on each drop, and every 14 seconds anyway.
  8. Chromatic splitThe red, green and blue layers separate into coloured fringes, like a VHS or 3D-glasses glitch. Music: they jump apart on each kick, wider for bigger kicks, then slide back.
  9. Colour bleedDrains the picture to black and white, then on each kick a ring of colour spreads out from the middle with a ragged, inky edge, like dye soaking into paper, and slowly fades back to grey. A new kick starts a fresh ring while the last one is still spreading, so busy beats keep the picture in colour and breaks let it drain away. Music: each kick starts a ring (harder kicks, stronger colour), the rings spread faster with the mids, and a drop floods out an extra-strong one.
  10. Colour by frequencyRecolours the picture in three rings: the middle takes the bass colour, the ring around it the mids, and the outside the hi-hats, keeping the picture's light and shade. On Vertex Shader the rings line up perfectly. Music: each ring glows brighter with its own part of the sound; drops turn the colours round the wheel.
  11. Colour cycleRecolours the whole picture through six colour moods in turn: sunset, ocean, ultraviolet, emerald, gold and rose ice. Each mood holds for about 20 seconds, then the next one blooms out from the middle like ink spreading through water, with a softly glowing edge, over about 6 seconds. It works on every background and over the other effects (except the Retro ones, which use their own palettes). Music: the change spreads a little quicker when the mids are busy, the glowing edge brightens on the bass, and a drop starts the next change straight away.
  12. Colour dropletsDrops of colour land at random spots. Each one sends out a ripple that bends the picture, with a bloom of ink-like colour that fades over about two seconds. Music: a drop lands on each kick, and the odd one falls when it's quiet. Up to six can be spreading at once.
  13. Comet streaksThe bright parts streak outward from the centre like a zoom blur, then draw back. Looks best on sparkly or dotty backgrounds such as Vertex Shader, Radio Storm and Line Fractals. Music: the streaks jump out on each kick, longer on harder ones.
  14. Counter-twistThe middle of the picture turns one way while the outside turns the other, then they swing back, so shapes weave through each other. Try it with Changing symmetry. Music: weaves faster when the mids (vocals, synths) are busy.
  15. Dichroic MirrorDichroic glass is coated so it lets one band of colours through and reflects the opposite band, and the colours change with the angle you see it from, which is why dichroic art and buildings shimmer gold, magenta and cyan as you walk past. Here three panes of it drift slowly across the picture: inside each you see the picture in the colour the glass passes, overlaid with a mirrored, slightly offset reflection in the complementary colour. Each pane slowly tilts, sliding its colours round the spectrum, and where panes overlap their filters stack into deeper colours. Music: the mids speed the drift, the bass tilts the panes so their colours sweep, the kick and hi-hats glint off the glass edges, and a drop flips every pane so the passed and reflected colours swap.
  16. Drops that landMakes drops dramatic: a coloured flash, the colours jump halfway round the wheel, a surge forward and a half-turn roll. Music: fires on each drop. With it off, drops still do their quieter changes.
  17. Glass orbsRay-traced glass floating over the picture. Each pixel of an orb follows a ray of light into the glass, bends, crosses it and bends again on the way out, so the background shows through upside down and curved like a crystal ball, with rainbow fringes where the colours split, a reflection round the edge, a bright highlight and a soft shadow on the picture beneath. It moves between one big sphere, three smaller ones, a cut crystal and three crystals. Works on every background. Music: the orbs drift, faster when the mids are busy, and swell a little on each kick; the rainbow fringes grow with the hi-hats; a drop moves on to the next arrangement.
  18. Gooch shadingThe illustrator's lighting: instead of light and dark, the sides of shapes facing a lamp turn warm and the sides turned away turn cool, with dark ink outlines round everything, like a screen-printed poster or a cel-shaded game. The warm and cool pair starts as orange and blue and slowly drifts round the colour wheel. Music: the lamp circles faster with the beat, the outlines thicken on the kick, and a drop swaps warm and cool so lit sides go cool and shadows go warm.
  19. Infrared filmThe false colours of infrared slide film such as Kodak Aerochrome, made for spotting camouflage and loved by psychedelic photographers. The film sees infrared as red, red as green and green as blue, so leaves and anything green or cyan glow hot pink and red, reds turn yellow-green and blues sink dark, with a red halation blooming round the brightest parts and a fine grain. Music: the bass blooms the halation, the hi-hats fizz the grain, and a drop flares the halation.
  20. Isometric reliefTurns the picture into a 3D landscape seen from above at an angle, drawn in parallel (isometric) projection like a strategy game or architect's drawing: the brightness becomes height, so bright parts rise as plateaus and towers and dark parts sink into valleys, lit from one side. It switches between a city of little coloured blocks and a smooth rolling terrain. Works on every background; busy, high-contrast ones like Neon Village, Dark City and Liquid Light Show make the best cities. It is heavier than the other effects, so turn it off if things stutter. Music: the view slowly drifts round the landscape and the blocks rise and sink smoothly as the picture changes; on each kick the landscape swells taller, brightens and catches up with the picture faster, and busy mids speed up the orbit; a drop swings the view round to the other side and swaps between city and terrain.
  21. Journey modeTours the backgrounds by itself. The old one swirls and drips as it melts into the next, over about 2½ seconds. Music: moves on every 32 bars (about 128 kicks), after 90 seconds if there's no clear beat, or on a big drop once 30 seconds have passed.
  22. Lambertian reflectanceTurns the picture into an embossed relief: its brightness is read as a height map and lit by a lamp circling the screen, so every shape gets a lit side and a shaded side, like carved clay or pressed metal, while keeping its colours. Music: the lamp circles faster with the beat and glows stronger on the kick; a drop swings it to the opposite side, so the depth seems to flip.
  23. Liquid wobbleThe whole picture breathes and ripples like heat haze over a road. Music: ripples harder on each kick.
  24. Living cellsThe picture seen through a living membrane, like a microscope slide, a dragonfly's wing or reptile scales: a network of glowing cell walls covers it, and each cell bulges gently like a bubble, brighter at its crown. Unlike Stained glass, the picture itself stays fully visible underneath. Suits darker, organic backgrounds like Deep Sea Fractal, Perlin Noise and Turing Garden. Music: the cells drift and wobble, faster with the mids; their walls flare brighter and thicker on each kick; their crowns glint with the hi-hats; a drop sets a fresh layout of cells.
  25. LuminanceLuminance is the light given off by an area, travelling in a given direction. This works out each part of the picture’s luminance using the CIE’s standard weights for how bright the eye sees red, green and blue (green counts most, blue least), and the bright parts then give off light: a soft glow spreads around them, and beams of their light travel across the picture in one direction, which slowly turns. Music: brighter on the bass, the hi-hats let dimmer parts glow too, and a drop swings the beams round a quarter turn with a flare.
  26. MarblingPushes the picture through flowing fractal noise that is folded back through itself (domain warping), so shapes swirl and curl like marbled paper, ink dropped in water or oil on a puddle. Unlike Liquid wobble, which is a regular ripple, the swirls are organic and never repeat. Best on bold, simple backgrounds like Spectrum Mandala, Neon Kaleidoscope and Fractal Tree, which melt into psychedelic marble. Music: the swirls flow faster when the mids are busy and run deeper on each kick; a drop stirs it hard before it settles.
  27. Matte BlackEverything as if it were coated in matte black, like a matte-wrapped car or stealth paint. The picture’s brightness is read as raised shapes on a black ground, and a broad, soft light picks them out only with a velvety grey sheen that rolls over their curves and edges, with no shine or hard highlights, and just a whisper of their original colour in it. Music: the soft lamp circles faster with the mids, the bass lifts the sheen, and a drop sends a band of light gliding across the surfaces, like a light passing over the bodywork.
  28. Melting dripsBright colours run down the screen in streaks like wet paint. Dark areas stay clean. Music: drips get longer on heavy bass.
  29. Melting trailsEach frame is laid over the last one, slightly zoomed and twisted, so shapes smear outward like melting wax. Music: trails stretch longer on the bass.
  30. MorphThe picture flows through four shapes: a swirl, a pinch to the centre, a fisheye bulge and a wave. It holds each one for a while, then blends into the next, roughly every 20 seconds. Music: the kick deepens the current shape, and a drop jumps to the next.
  31. Neon contoursTurns the picture into a glowing line drawing: every sharp change in the picture is traced as a neon line in its own colour, and everything else is dimmed right down, so blobs become outlines, fractals become line art and the dot fields look like wireframes. Music: the lines thicken on the kick and shift colour with the mids; a drop flashes the full picture back, then it fades to lines again.
  32. Noir InkThe graphic-novel noir look made famous by Sin City, which was shot in colour and turned into inked comic panels afterwards. The picture is crushed into hard black and white with only a sliver of grey between them, and one colour is kept, flat and vivid, the way blood, a red dress or a pair of eyes stand out in the comic. Red is kept most of the time, giving way to yellow or blue for a minute now and then. Music: the kick flares the kept colour and pushes more of the picture into white, and a drop flips the panel to white silhouettes on black for a moment, like the film’s most striking shots.
  33. Oblique projectionThe cabinet drawing an architect or comic artist uses for 3D lettering: the picture stays flat and face-on, so you can still see exactly what it is, but its bright parts stand out towards you as solid shapes with sides running back diagonally into the screen. Dark areas stay flat at the back. Best on backgrounds with clear bright shapes, like Neon Kaleidoscope, Liquid Light Show, Spectrum Mandala and Neon Village. Music: the shapes stand out further on each kick and the direction the sides run slowly sways; a drop swings it round a quarter turn to the next corner.
  34. Oren–Nayar reflectanceThe rough-surface cousin of Lambertian reflectance. The picture is again read as a height map and lit by a circling lamp, but as a rough matte surface (clay, chalk, velvet or moon dust) with a fine powdery grain. Rough surfaces throw light back towards where it came from, which is why the full Moon looks like a flat disc: so when the lamp climbs high the relief melts into an even, powdery glow, and as it sinks low the shapes stand out again with soft, dusty shadows. The lamp slowly rises and sinks the whole time. Music: the lamp circles faster with the mids, the bass roughens the surface, the kick brightens it, and a drop swings the lamp to the other side and drops it low so the relief leaps out.
  35. Pencil shadingThe picture redrawn as a graphite pencil drawing on paper. The light parts are left as bare paper, and the tones are built up the way an artist shades: one direction of hatching for the mid-tones, crossing layers for the darker parts and a soft smudge for the very darkest, with wavy, broken strokes whose pressure varies along them, sketchy contour lines round the shapes and the paper’s tooth catching the graphite unevenly. The strokes re-draw several times a second, the way an animated pencil drawing “boils”. Music: the bass deepens the shading, and a drop re-draws all the hatching at a new angle.
  36. Quantum SpinThe wagon-wheel effect from old films, where fast-turning spokes seem to creep backwards. Turn it on with Changing symmetry: the picture inside the kaleidoscope races round ten times faster than Spinning, while the kaleidoscope's pattern itself crawls slowly the other way, so the eye sees both directions at once. It does this smoothly rather than by flickering, so there is no strobing. Without Changing symmetry it is simply a very fast spin. If more than one spin effect is on, the fastest wins. Music: the pattern lurches forwards on each kick before crawling back, the picture spins faster on the bass, and a drop reverses everything.
  37. Retro 1-BitEvery pixel is either black or white, like an old Mac or a photocopy, with dither patterns making the greys. Glowing, busy backgrounds turn into stark graphic patterns. Music: the pixels get chunkier on each kick and snap back, the dither pattern fizzes with the hi-hats, and a drop bursts the pixels big for a moment. Switch on more than one Retro effect and they take turns, swapping at a drop or about every 16 bars.
  38. Retro CGAThe look of early-80s IBM PC games: big square pixels in just four colours, black, cyan, magenta and white, with dither patterns mixing them for everything in between. Loud, clashing and unmistakably retro. Music: the pixels get chunkier on each kick and snap back, the dither pattern fizzes with the hi-hats, and a drop bursts the pixels big for a moment. Switch on more than one Retro effect and they take turns, swapping at a drop or about every 16 bars.
  39. Retro Commodore 64Big square pixels in the Commodore 64's muted 16 colours (browns, olive, dusty blue and purple, pastel green), with dither patterns for the shades in between, like an 80s home-computer game. Music: the pixels get chunkier on each kick and snap back, the dither pattern fizzes with the hi-hats, and a drop bursts the pixels big for a moment. Switch on more than one Retro effect and they take turns, swapping at a drop or about every 16 bars.
  40. Retro PICO-8The look of the PICO-8 fantasy console: the picture is drawn in big square pixels and snapped to its bright 16-colour palette (navy, plum, forest green, orange, hot pink, sky blue, yellow, peach and greys), with checkerboard-style dither patterns standing in for the shades in between. The most colourful of the Retro effects and the closest to the original background. Music: the pixels get chunkier on each kick and snap back, the dither pattern fizzes with the hi-hats, and a drop bursts the pixels big for a moment. Switch on more than one Retro effect and they take turns, swapping at a drop or about every 16 bars.
  41. RisographThe look of the risograph-printed flyers and zines of the 90s rave scene. The picture is split into three translucent spot inks, pink, blue and yellow (or orange, teal and purple, swapping every 50 seconds), each printed in a grainy dot slightly out of register with the others on cream paper, so the colours overlap and fringe like the real thing. Dark scenes come out as mostly bare paper with ink where the light is. Music: the layers drift further apart on the bass, and a drop knocks them well out of register before they settle back.
  42. Saturation pulseTurns the strength of the colours up and down with the music. Quiet passages and breaks drain the picture to soft, washed-out pastels, close to grey; as the music builds the colour floods back in, and busy, bass-heavy parts push it richer than normal. Music: follows the bass and mids, with a little bump on each kick; a drop slams the colour fully vivid for a moment before it eases back.
  43. Selective colourThe film trick: the whole picture goes black and white except for one band of colour, which stays vivid, so only the reds, or only the blues, glow out of a grey scene. The kept colour drifts slowly right round the wheel, about once every 90 seconds, so different parts of each background take turns to light up. Music: the band of kept colour widens a little on the bass, and a drop jumps it to a new colour with a flash.
  44. ShimmerA fine heat-haze ripple, glitter twinkling on the brightest parts and a faint oil-slick sheen over the highlights. Music: more glitter and livelier ripples on the hi-hats.
  45. ShockwaveA ripple rings out from the centre, bending the picture as it passes, with a bright coloured edge. It takes about a second and a half to fade. Music: fires on every drop, and on a big kick (at most every 4 seconds).
  46. Silver retentionAlso called bleach bypass: the film-lab trick, used on films like Saving Private Ryan and Se7en, of skipping the bleach so the silver stays in the print. The silver lays a black-and-white copy over the colour, so the colours drain, the contrast hardens, the blacks go inky and the highlights take on a cool, metallic sheen, with a fine film grain that is strongest in the mid-tones. How much silver is kept drifts slowly, as if each print were developed a little differently. Music: the bass adds silver, the kick glints off the highlights, the hi-hats fizz the grain, and a drop floods the picture to full silver before it eases back.
  47. SmokeDrifting clouds of fractal smoke roll across the picture, like a smoke machine at a gig. The picture glows through the thin parts and is softened behind the thick parts, and the smoke picks up the colours of whatever is lighting it from behind. Made for pairing with Volumetric light, whose beams look like light through smoke. Music: the smoke drifts faster with the mids and billows thicker on each kick; a drop puffs out a burst.
  48. SolariseThe darkroom trick known as the Sabattier effect, used on many psychedelic album photos: anything brighter than a cut-off is flipped to its negative, so dark areas stay dark while highlights turn into glowing negative outlines with an eerie metallic edge. Each colour is flipped on its own, as in the darkroom, which brings odd colour shifts. Music: the bass lowers the cut-off, so more of the picture flips on the kick, and a drop flashes the full negative, easing back over about a second.
  49. SpinningThe view turns slowly and steadily. Music: turns faster on the bass and reverses direction on each drop.
  50. Stained glassTurns the picture into a leaded church window using cellular texturing: it is broken into irregular panes, each glowing with one rich colour taken from the picture at that spot, brightest at its heart, with dark lead lines between and glints of light sweeping across the old, uneven glass. The background stays recognisable, just made of glass. Best on colourful backgrounds like Neon Kaleidoscope, Spectrum Mandala and Liquid Light Show, and lovely with Volumetric light. Music: the panes drift slowly, faster when the mids are busy; the glass brightens and the lead thickens on each kick; glints flash with the hi-hats; a drop re-leads the window in a new pattern.
  51. Triple SpinThe same as Spinning but three times as fast, about one turn every 12 seconds when quiet. On its own the pattern stays the same, just turning faster; with Melting trails on, each frame smears round behind the last and the picture swirls into a vortex. If more than one spin effect is on, the fastest wins. Music: turns faster on the bass and reverses direction on each drop.
  52. Two-tone cycleLike Colour cycle, but instead of one colour mood it splits the picture between two contrasting colours: magenta and cyan, teal and orange, violet and lime, blue and red, purple and gold, then green and pink. Which colour each part takes depends on both how bright it is and which side of the colour wheel it was on, so darker, cooler parts take one and brighter, warmer parts the other. Each pair holds for about 20 seconds, then the next blooms out from the middle like ink, with a softly glowing edge. Music: the change spreads quicker when the mids are busy, the glowing edge brightens on the bass, and a drop starts the next change straight away.
  53. Volumetric lightLight shafts, or "god rays": the picture looks lit through smoke or haze, like stage lights through a smoke machine or sunlight through trees. A light drifts slowly around the screen and beams pour from it through the gaps, while dark shapes block it and cast long shadow rays. Unlike Comet streaks, which only bursts on the kick, the haze is there all the time. Best on backgrounds with strong shapes, like Fractal Tree, Neon Kaleidoscope, Spectrum Mandala and Dark City. Music: the beams brighten and reach further on each kick, the light drifts faster when the mids are busy, and a drop moves it somewhere new with a flash.
  54. Ward anisotropicAdds a glossy sheen whose highlights are stretched into streaks along a grain, like brushed metal, satin or the grooves of a vinyl record. The grain runs in rings round the centre, so a bright highlight cross sweeps round the picture as its lamp circles. Pairs well with Lambertian reflectance: one gives the shading, the other the shine. Music: the lamp circles faster with the beat, the streaks stretch longer on the bass and brighten on the hi-hats; a drop swaps the record rings for straight brushed-metal lines (and back).

Tips

  • Good starting mix: Melting trails, Liquid wobble and Drops that land.
  • Busy combinations: Morph with Melting drips, or lots of warps at once, can get very intense. Fewer effects often look better.
  • Full screen: use the button, or double-click the visuals. Press Esc or double-click again to leave.
  • Remembered settings: your background and effects are saved for next time.
  • Slower computers: the page lowers its resolution by itself to keep things smooth.
